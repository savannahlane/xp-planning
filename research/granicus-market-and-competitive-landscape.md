# Granicus: Market Footprint, Competitive Landscape, and Procurement Vehicles

**Prepared:** September 2026
**Purpose:** Input to a state-by-state territory tiering exercise.
**Evidence standard:** Every factual claim below is tied to a numbered source in Section 7. Claims that are inference rather than sourced fact are explicitly labelled **[INFERENCE]**. Where a source is a vendor's own marketing, it is labelled as such, because vendor self-reported counts are unaudited.

---

## Narrative summary

Granicus is a large, private-equity-owned, government-only SaaS company with an unusually wide product surface and an unusually *shallow* footprint in the single product category people assume it dominates. Its own marketing claims 7,000+ public sector organizations worldwide, 350M+ opt-in subscribers, 48 of the 50 largest U.S. cities, and 100% of U.S. federal cabinet-level agencies [1][2][6][15]. Reuters reported roughly $175M EBITDA and a targeted ~$4B enterprise value in a sale process that Vista Equity Partners and Harvest Partners launched in 2025 [4]; as of this writing no completed sale has been reported, and PitchBook still classifies the latest deal signal as rumor/speculation [30].

The strategically important fact for territory planning is that **Granicus's saturation is concentrated at the top of the market and in specific product lines, not across the local-government long tail.** Granicus's own public-records marketing claims 110+ of the top 300 U.S. cities, 45% of top U.S. counties, and 80+ state agencies [15] — i.e., strength in large jurisdictions. By contrast, an independent 2026 scan of the entire `.gov` registrant set found CivicPlus running 20.5% of reachable U.S. government websites (2,464 sites) and Revize 6.3% (755), against Granicus at 1.1% (138) [21]. Even allowing for the methodology's blind spots (see caveats in Section 3), the direction is clear: in the local-government **website CMS** category, Granicus is a distant challenger to CivicPlus, while in **communications (govDelivery)**, **records requests (GovQA)**, and **large-jurisdiction meeting/agenda**, it is the incumbent.

Whitespace therefore is not evenly distributed by geography so much as by **jurisdiction size crossed with product line**. The realistic expansion motion in most states is (a) cross-sell into existing large-jurisdiction accounts, and (b) reach the mid-market through cooperative and statewide vehicles, since the long tail of small jurisdictions cannot run its own procurement (Section 5).

On procurement, Granicus has one strong *direct* national cooperative vehicle — OMNIA Partners / Region 14 ESC contract **159768**, running December 8, 2025 through September 30, 2028 with options to September 30, 2030 [24], the successor to legacy NCPA 01-115 [23]. Almost everything else in the widely circulated "Granicus contract list" is actually held by **Carahsoft**, its reseller, not by Granicus [8]. That distinction matters for margin and for states like Virginia, whose IT procurement policy actively discourages cooperative piggybacking when a VITA statewide contract exists [28].

The clearest statewide anchors found with hard documentation are **New York** (state-awarded GovQA contract, ~$650K/year, in use by 63 of 72 agencies as of May 2026) [11][12], **Michigan** (MiDEAL contract MA071B7700199C, in force since 2017 and extended to September 30, 2027, ~$7.22M aggregate, open to Michigan locals) [13], and **Oklahoma** (OMES statewide SW1041C running to October 2030, in a state whose central purchasing office actively markets statewide contracts to cities) [8][9]. Washington and Montana both have documented Granicus statewide master agreements, but the public records for both show terms that appear to have lapsed and should be re-verified before being counted [31][32][33].

The single biggest competitive threat by structure, not by product, is **Tyler Technologies**: its 2025 10-K reports dedicated state-level offices in the 30 states where it holds enterprise contracts [17], a distribution advantage no other vendor in this space has. The single biggest *channel* threat is the **state municipal league endorsement**, which Granicus does not appear to hold anywhere; VC3 holds 11 league endorsements across 10 states covering 5,493 municipalities [20].

## 2. Statewide and enterprise-wide state agreements

This is the section where marketing language and contractual reality diverge most, so each entry below states what the document actually says.

### Well-documented statewide Granicus agreements

**New York — GovQA / Records Request Management. Strongest current evidence.**
Granicus LLC was awarded a State of New York contract in March 2022 for the FOIL software GovQA, at **$650,000 annually**, with scope requirements including a portal appeals feature, ad hoc reporting, and no-cost data export [11][12]. As of a May 2026 Reinvent Albany review, **63 of 72 state agencies surveyed were accepting FOIL requests through GovQA** [12]. This is effectively a statewide records-request standard. Governor Hochul publicly promoted the contract [12].

**Michigan — MiDEAL, govDelivery/Digital Communications.**
Contract **MA071B7700199C** with Granicus, LLC, titled "Digital Communications – Mass Text Message Solutions," initial effective date **September 30, 2017**, estimated aggregate contract value **$7,223,422.34**, extended by Change Notice 29 (August 2026) with a one-year option increase of $664,076.25 and a **revised expiration of September 30, 2027** [13]. Statements of work in the file are written against MDHHS and LARA and explicitly state "Procurement Vehicle: MiDeal" [13]. **MiDEAL is Michigan's extended purchasing program open to local units of government**, which makes this both a state-agency and a local-government vehicle.

**Oklahoma — OMES statewide.**
Granicus is available under Oklahoma OMES statewide contract **SW1041C**, October 26, 2023 through **October 26, 2030** — the longest-dated vehicle in the set — and explicitly available to Oklahoma state, local, and education entities, and to non-Oklahoma SLED entities with prior approval [8]. Separately, Oklahoma OMES Central Purchasing runs a recurring outreach program specifically encouraging **all Oklahoma cities** to buy from its 200+ statewide contracts [9]. **[INFERENCE]** The combination of a long-dated vehicle and an active state-run demand-generation channel for cities makes Oklahoma unusually low-friction relative to its size.

**Iowa — two state master agreements.**
State of Iowa **2019-BUS-228**, "Granicus Amanda Software, Services and Equipment," with the vendor listed as Granicus, Inc. and jurisdiction covering **All State Agencies** and **Other Governmental Entities** [10]. Plus a newer **2026-BUS-7713** COTS contract (via Carahsoft, based on TX DIR-CPO-5687), May 19, 2026 – May 19, 2027 with option years, available to Iowa state, local, and education entities [8].

### Documented but currency-uncertain — verify before relying on

**Washington — DES statewide contract 01313.**
Washington Department of Enterprise Services statewide contract **01313, "Digital Communication Solutions," contractor Granicus LLC (formerly GovDelivery)**, with published pricing tiers for both state agencies and non-state agencies, the latter requiring a **Master Contracts Usage Agreement (MCUA)** [31][32]. Washington-led cooperative contracts are also usable by **out-of-state** government entities that sign a cooperative usage agreement [33]. **Caveat:** the DES contract summary page shows Effective Date 11-01-2018 with "Current Term Ends On: 11-15-2019" and "Final Term Ends On: 11-15-2019" [31]. Either the page is stale or the contract has lapsed. Do not present this as live without confirming with DES.

**Montana — state MSA with GovDelivery/Granicus.**
A fully executed Master Services Agreement between the State of Montana, Department of Administration, SITSD and **GovDelivery, LLC d/b/a Granicus** is published on state servers [14]. Critically, **Section 18, Cooperative Purchasing**, extends the agreement's prices, terms, and conditions to "public procurement units" as defined in 18-4-401, MCA — which expressly includes **local or state public procurement units of this or any other state**, U.S. agencies, and tribal procurement units [14]. **Caveat:** Section 1.1 sets the initial term through **January 31, 2021**, renewable under then-existing terms [14]. Verify current status.

### What is *not* Granicus's own paper

The frequently cited list of ~60 state and cooperative contracts covering Granicus is **Carahsoft's**, not Granicus's [8]. It includes Pennsylvania DGS 4400028357 (to Aug 2028), Ohio State Term Schedule 534354 (to Dec 2026), Maryland COTS Master 060B2490021 (to Sept 2027), California CMAS 3-20-70-2247G (to Aug 2028), New Mexico 30-00000-23-00067 (to Apr 2027), State of Florida OMNIA/Cobb 43210000-23-OMNIA-ACS (to Apr 2028), Texas DIR-CPO-5687 (to May 2027, expiration May 2031), TIPS 220105, VASCUPP UVA1482501, MHEC MC15-F04, and NASPO ValuePoint Cloud Solutions participating addenda in roughly 28–30 states [8][25].

Granicus's own former Texas DIR contract, **DIR-TSO-3631**, is now marked **Inactive** by Texas DIR [26]. Granicus today reaches Texas buyers through Carahsoft's DIR-CPO-5687, on which Granicus is one of ~300 listed brands [26].

**[INFERENCE]** Reseller-mediated vehicles are strategically weaker than direct vehicles: they add margin leakage, they put Granicus on a list with hundreds of competing brands rather than as a named awardee, and they do not create a state-level relationship. The OMNIA 159768 award and the direct state MSAs (MI, MT, WA, NY, IA, OK) are the assets worth defending.

### Statewide website programs

No evidence was found of Granicus operating a statewide *website program* for local governments in any state. Granicus does have flagship state-agency website work — e.g. the Virginia Department of Environmental Quality site built with the Granicus Experience Group, and Arizona DEQ [29] — and OpenCities powers large city sites including Miami, Syracuse, Orlando, and Denver [22]. Statewide *portal* programs are overwhelmingly Tyler/NIC territory (Section 3).

## 3. Competitive landscape by product area and geography

### 3.1 Website / CMS — Granicus is not the incumbent

The most useful independent data point is the 2026 Replatform Radar study "Nobody Voted for This Template," which fetched the entire U.S. `.gov`/`.mil` registrant set (18,682 domains, 14,001 reachable) and fingerprinted platforms [21]:

| Vendor | Sites | Share of reachable U.S. government sites |
|---|---|---|
| WordPress | 2,493 | 20.7% |
| **CivicPlus** | 2,464 | **20.5%** |
| Drupal | 759 | 6.3% |
| **Revize** | 755 | **6.3%** |
| Wix | 388 | 3.2% |
| Munibit | 184 | 1.5% |
| Catalis | 167 | 1.4% |
| **Granicus** | 138 | **1.1%** |
| MunicipalImpact | 131 | 1.1% |
| Tyler Technologies | 51 | 0.4% |
| Streamline | 48 | 0.4% |
| Municode | 37 | 0.3% |
| Rock Solid / QScend | 25 | 0.2% |

**Read this carefully before using it.** Four caveats, all from the study's own method section [21]: (a) the population is **`.gov`/`.mil` only**, so the very large number of local governments on `.org`, `.com`, or `.us` domains is invisible — this almost certainly understates every specialist vendor, and plausibly understates Granicus more than CivicPlus if Granicus skews to large jurisdictions with established `.gov` domains, or less if it skews the other way; (b) attribution succeeded on only 71.2% of reachable sites, so all shares are floors; (c) **embedded third-party services including meeting portals are recorded separately and never counted as the site's vendor**, which specifically excludes Granicus's very large Legistar/agenda footprint from these numbers; (d) Version 2 of the study corrected a major attribution error (Municode fell from 512 sites to 37), which is a good sign for methodological honesty but also a reminder that fingerprinting is fragile.

Even discounted heavily, the CivicPlus-versus-Granicus gap in local-government CMS is large and directionally reliable.

**CivicPlus scale:** 12,000+ customers per its own LinkedIn profile, ~12,500 per AppsRunTheWorld, 13,000+ per a third-party directory citing CivicPlus's claims, with 100,000+ local government users and 340M+ residents in the U.S. and Canada [18]. Owned by Insight Partners since 2021. Acquisition roll-up: **Municode** (announced 2021, closed/announced publicly 2023 depending on source) — the largest codifier of local legal documents in the U.S., headquartered in Tallahassee, Florida; **Optimere** (ArchiveSocial + Monsido + **NextRequest**) in May 2022, ~6,000 customers and ~$30M ARR pre-deal; **SeeClickFix**; **Beehive Industries** (Jan 2025) for asset management and utility billing; **PASS Lite App** (2024) [18].

**Revize:** headquartered in Troy, Michigan; claims 3,000 government clients nationwide (2,600 in slightly older materials) [19]. Second-largest government-specialist CMS fleet in the `.gov` scan [21]. **[INFERENCE]** Revize's reference list skews heavily Michigan and Midwest (Albion MI, Genesee County MI, Farmington DDA MI, Tinley Park IL, Glencoe IL, Des Moines IA, Cedar Rapids IA), which is consistent with a Michigan-HQ vendor that grew regionally before going national [19]. Treat Michigan/Illinois/Iowa as Revize-defended territory in the small-to-mid city website segment.

**Streamline:** Sacramento-based, purpose-built for **California independent special districts**, whose website obligation is statutorily mandated by **SB 929** (Gov. Code §53087.8, effective January 1, 2020) with only an annually renewable hardship exemption [27]. Streamline partners with the Special District Leadership Foundation on scholarships for the smallest districts [27]. California has **2,949 special-purpose districts other than school districts** [35], so this is a defensible regulatory-driven niche. Streamline appears at only 48 `.gov` sites [21], consistent with small districts rarely holding `.gov` domains.

### 3.2 Digital government portals and payments — Tyler/NIC owns the state layer

Tyler Technologies' 2025 10-K (filed February 2026) reports ~**$2.3B revenue**, 87% recurring, **45,000+ installations across ~15,000 locations**, clients in all 50 states, and — the load-bearing fact — **"dedicated state-level offices in the 30 states in which we have enterprise contracts"** [17]. Tyler's own state-industry brochure says **28 states** with statewide enterprise contracts for digital government [17]. Tyler Fast Facts 2025: 22 of the 25 largest U.S. counties and **25 of the 25 largest U.S. cities** are clients; 98% retention [17].

Tyler's NIC subsidiary structure names state-specific entities for: Alabama, Arkansas, Colorado, Connecticut, Delaware, Florida, Hawaii, Idaho, Illinois, Indiana, Iowa, Kansas, Kentucky, Louisiana, Maine, Maryland, Michigan, Mississippi, Montana, Nebraska, New Jersey, New Mexico, New York, North Carolina, Oklahoma, Oregon, Pennsylvania, Rhode Island, South Carolina, South Dakota, Tennessee, Texas, Utah, Vermont, Virginia, West Virginia, and Wisconsin [17]. **[INFERENCE]** The subsidiary list is a *historical* footprint marker, not a list of currently active portal contracts — Tyler's own count of 28–30 is lower than the ~37 entities listed, so some are dormant. Use the subsidiary list to identify where Tyler has state-government relationship infrastructure, not to assert an active contract.

The NIC model matters competitively: in Hawaii, Vermont, and elsewhere, NIC/Tyler operates the state portal on a **self-funded, transaction-fee model at no cost to the state**, and in Vermont extends that to "more than 90 state government agencies and localities" [17]. **[INFERENCE]** A free-to-the-agency channel into local governments is a structurally harder thing to displace than a competing paid product, and it is the most under-appreciated competitive risk to Granicus in NIC states.

### 3.3 Permitting, licensing, ERP

**OpenGov:** Cox Enterprises took majority ownership in February 2024 at a **$1.8B valuation**; nearly 1,900 customers in all 50 states at deal time, **2,000+ agencies** by 2025–26 [36]. Product breadth now spans ERP, HCM, budgeting, permitting, procurement, asset management (Cartegraph), and utility billing, with OG Assist AI announced April 2026 [36]. OpenGov overlaps Granicus in permitting/licensing and in transparency, and is the more aggressive mid-market challenger.

**Accela:** ~2,200–2,500 government agencies globally; strongest in permitting-first and large urban jurisdictions [37]. One 2026 competitive analysis characterizes Accela as having "deep roots in California, Florida, and large counties" and "a stronger presence on the West Coast, particularly California, and in major metro areas," while Tyler EnerGov/Enterprise Permitting & Licensing (700+ agencies) wins mid-size cities on ERP integration [37]. Treat the regional characterization as **[INFERENCE]-grade** — it comes from a vendor-intelligence blog, not a primary source.

### 3.4 Records requests

Granicus (GovQA) claims the largest public-records customer base, with the large-jurisdiction figures cited in Section 1 [15]. **CivicPlus NextRequest** claims 70K+ active users and ~1 million requests per year processed [38]. **JustFOIA** competes on simplicity and published low pricing ($99–$499/month, no contract, no implementation fee) — a genuinely different go-to-market aimed squarely at the small jurisdictions Granicus struggles to reach economically [38]. **Laserfiche, OnBase/Hyland, and Accela** compete as horizontal ECM platforms where records management is a component rather than the product [38].

New York is the clearest statewide records win in the country and it belongs to Granicus [11][12].

### 3.5 Resident engagement (vs. EngagementHQ)

**Polco** merged with **Balancing Act** in 2023; Polco had ~500 local government clients, Balancing Act 150+ [39]. **Zencity** acquired UK-based **Commonplace** in January 2025; Zencity reports **400+ government agencies** and claims 8 of the 10 largest U.S. cities [39]. **PublicInput** (Raleigh, NC) is smaller, with roughly 300 customers and ~$2.9M revenue by third-party estimate [39]. **Important correction to a common assumption:** there was no Polco–Zencity merger; those are separate companies and separate transactions [39].

### 3.6 Short-term rental compliance

A genuinely fragmented category with no public share data. Competitors to Granicus Host Compliance: **Deckard Technologies (Rentalscape)**, **GovOS** (which absorbed **LODGINGRevs**), and **Avenu Insights & Analytics** [40]. Two useful primary-source data points: the Town of Cape Charles, Virginia ran an RFP in 2025, shortlisted **GovOS, Avenu, and Deckard** — Granicus did not make the final three — and selected Deckard at **$32,000 for year one** [40]. A Seminole County, Florida evaluation scored Avenu and Deckard head-to-head, with a note that Deckard's Florida subsidiary HERA already had 40 Florida community customers [40]. **[INFERENCE]** Granicus's absence from both shortlists is a single-data-point signal, not proof of decline, but Host Compliance faces real price and feature pressure in the Southeast.

## 4. League endorsements, county programs, and cooperative purchasing

### 4.1 State municipal league endorsements — the channel Granicus does not have

**VC3** is the clearest worked example of the endorsement model in govtech. It states it is "an endorsed partner for 5,493 municipalities across 10 states" with **11 municipal league partner endorsements** [20]. Named endorsers:

| League | Member jurisdictions | Relationship start (where stated) |
|---|---|---|
| Alabama League of Municipalities | 450+ | — |
| League of California Cities | 476 | — |
| California Special Districts Association (CSDA) | 1,300+ | — |
| Connecticut Conference of Municipalities | 168 | — |
| Georgia Municipal Association | 537 | 2012 |
| Iowa League of Cities | 850+ | 2018 |
| Maryland Municipal League | 160 | — |
| North Carolina League of Municipalities | 540 | 2007 |
| Municipal Association of South Carolina | 271 | 1999 |
| Tennessee Municipal League | 345 | 2017 (league's own IT) |
| League of Wisconsin Municipalities | — | 2024 |

Source: VC3 local government partners page [20]. The Wisconsin league goes further than endorsement — it lets VC3 **apply for State and Local Cybersecurity Grant Program funding on members' behalf** [20], which is about as deep as a channel relationship gets.

At the national level, the **NLC Strategic Partner** program has three tiers, with **Enterprise Partners** being "endorsed" and carrying a revenue-sharing arrangement with NLC; Tyler Technologies appears in the NLC catalog, and eSCRIBE received an exclusive NLC Enterprise Partner endorsement for meeting management and citizen engagement in 2022 [20].

**[INFERENCE] and a strategic read:** no evidence was found of Granicus holding a state municipal league endorsement anywhere. In AL, CA, CT, GA, IA, MD, NC, SC, TN, and WI, a competitor already occupies the trusted-referral slot for technology, and a small-city buyer in those states will often start a technology conversation at the league rather than with a vendor. That does not block Granicus — VC3 sells managed IT and cybersecurity, not civic engagement SaaS, so the overlap is partial — but it means the warm-intro channel is taken.

### 4.2 County associations

**NACo EDGE / Public Promise Procurement** uses a **Lead Public Agency, single-award, peer-vetted** model, with contracts made public and reviewed by an advisory committee of procurement officials; Cobb County, Georgia has served as a lead public agency [41]. Single-award structure specifically satisfies piggybacking and compliance requirements [41]. NACo separately **endorses** specific products — ClearGov's Budget Cycle Management and ClearForms are endorsed, with ClearGov citing 900+ agencies including 140+ counties [41]. State county associations (e.g., Virginia Association of Counties, Minnesota Counties) actively promote these programs to members [41].

### 4.3 National cooperatives

| Vehicle | Reported membership | Positioning |
|---|---|---|
| **OMNIA Partners** | 86,000–90,000 member organizations (includes private sector) | Strongest in cities, counties, municipalities; absorbed National IPA, U.S. Communities, TCPN, KATA, NCPA [42][23] |
| **Sourcewell** | 50,000+ agency members, all 50 states + Canada; Minnesota statutory service cooperative | Broadest full-SLED national reach; free membership [42] |
| **TIPS** (Region 8 ESC, TX) | 4,500–8,500 depending on source | Texas/Southern education and government; strong in construction and JOC [42] |

| **NASPO ValuePoint** | Lead-state model across all states | State-led; Granicus reachable via Carahsoft participating addenda in ~28–30 states [8][25] |

**Granicus's own cooperative position:** OMNIA Partners / Region 14 ESC contract **159768**, initial term **December 8, 2025 – September 30, 2028**, with two one-year renewal options through **September 30, 2030** [24]. Predecessor **NCPA 01-115** (Region 14 ESC solicitation 39-20), awarded December 8, 2020, extended through December 31, 2025; NCPA was acquired by OMNIA Partners on December 6, 2022 [23]. Granicus's RFP response states it does **not** anticipate using distributors or resellers under 159768, delivering directly, while acknowledging it is "currently listed on reseller-held OMNIA contracts" [24].

**No Granicus Sourcewell award was found.** A Sourcewell contract-search check and multiple targeted searches returned nothing [22]. **[INFERENCE]** Given Sourcewell's 50,000-agency membership and its strength precisely in the small-to-mid jurisdictions where Granicus is weakest, this looks like the single largest unexploited channel gap in Granicus's procurement portfolio.

### 4.4 State-specific vehicles worth knowing

- **Texas DIR** — DIR-CPO-5687 (Carahsoft, Granicus a listed brand); usable by Texas SLED and by out-of-state public entities with prior approval [26]. Granicus's own DIR-TSO-3631 is inactive [26].
- **Virginia VITA** — by statute (Va. Code §2.2-2012(C)) **all VITA statewide IT contracts must provide for inclusion of counties, cities, and towns**, and localities and school divisions are authorized to buy from VITA and its vendors [28]. But VITA's Joint and Cooperative Procurement Policy states cooperative contracts **may not** be used for IT goods/services already available on a VITA statewide contract, and "typically should not be used for software purchases or ongoing service level agreements," with CIO approval required regardless of dollar amount [28]. VITA currently runs ~200 IT contracts, and use is **mandatory** for executive branch agencies [28]. **Translation: in Virginia, being on the VITA paper is close to a prerequisite; a cooperative workaround is disfavored by policy.**
- **California CMAS** — built on a GSA base schedule, **optional** for local agencies, authorized without competitive bidding by PCC §§10298 and 10299, but each local agency decides whether CMAS fits its own policy [43].
- **Pennsylvania COSTARS** — DGS-run cooperative open to local governments, schools, libraries, and nonprofits, with 100+ pre-negotiated vendor contracts; statutory authority in the Commonwealth Procurement Code [31]. Granicus reaches PA via Carahsoft DGS 4400028357 and NASPO 4400021360 [8].
- **Washington DES** — Washington-led cooperative contracts are available to **out-of-state** government entities that execute a cooperative contract usage agreement [33]. This makes any Washington-led Granicus award a multi-state asset, not just a Washington one.
- **Michigan MiDEAL** — extended purchasing for Michigan local units of government; the live Granicus vehicle [13].
- **Oklahoma OMES** — SW1041C to 2030, with OMES actively marketing statewide contracts to all Oklahoma cities [8][9].

## 5. States commonly described as difficult govtech markets

There is no authoritative published ranking of "hard govtech states." What exists is (a) hard Census fragmentation data and (b) qualitative practitioner commentary. Both are below; the synthesis is labelled inference.

### Fragmentation — hard data (2022 Census of Governments)

Total local governments: **90,837** in 2022, rising to **91,438** in 2025 [35]. Composition in 2022: 3,031 counties, 19,491 municipalities, 16,214 townships, 12,546 independent school districts, 39,555 special districts [35].

The St. Louis Fed's framing is the relevant one: Illinois has more than 1.5 times as many local governments as California despite under a third the population [35]. Townships exist in all counties in 11 states, and in Illinois 85 of 102 counties, Kansas 95 of 105, Minnesota 84 of 87, North Dakota 48 of 53, South Dakota 52 of 66, Wisconsin 71 of 72, and every New York county outside NYC [35].

### Capacity — qualitative but well-sourced

- Alan Shark of the Public Technology Institute estimates roughly **30,000 small local government units**, of which about **half have only one to three IT staff**; Pittsfield, Illinois employs **zero** IT staff and outsources entirely [44].
- Michigan: nearly 1,300 municipalities; most small jurisdictions are "lucky if they have a single employee designated to handle IT"; constitutional property-tax limits plus state revenue sharing that fell from ~$1.6B (2001) to $1.2B (2016) constrain IT budgets [44].
- North Dakota: of 53 counties, **only six employ full-time IT staff**; the rest rely on MSPs, informal consultants, or long-tenured generalists, compounded by a statutory hiring freeze and a 10% budget-cut mandate [44].
- North Carolina counties: a procurement-capacity gap means a county "may genuinely want to buy" and still be unable to move a procurement forward because no one internally can write a specification, run an RFP, evaluate responses, negotiate, and oversee implementation on top of their day job [44].
- SLED generally: 50 different state procurement codes plus thousands of local ordinances, no single registration portal, no standard solicitation format, no shared competitive-bidding threshold, and procurement cycles commonly 6–24 months [44].

### **[INFERENCE]** Synthesis: which states are structurally hardest

Combining fragmentation, township density, and capacity signals, the states that will most reward a **cooperative-first, low-touch, pre-priced** motion and most punish a **bespoke-RFP, enterprise-sales** motion are:

- **Illinois** — highest count nationally, 2,720 subcounty units plus 3,218 special districts, township-heavy, no Granicus statewide vehicle found. Hardest large state.
- **Pennsylvania** — 2,559 subcounty units, 37 governments per 100K people, township-heavy; COSTARS partially offsets this.
- **Ohio, Kansas, Minnesota, North Dakota, South Dakota, Wisconsin, New York (outside NYC)** — township structures create very large numbers of very small buyers.
- **North Dakota specifically** — documented near-total absence of county IT staff.
- **Rural interior West and Plains generally** — small jurisdiction size and thin staffing.

Conversely, **states with strong centralized IT procurement plus explicit local-government access** lower the cost of sale materially: Virginia (statutory local access to VITA contracts, but VITA-paper-only), Michigan (MiDEAL), Oklahoma (OMES with active city outreach), Washington (DES with MCUA plus out-of-state cooperative access), Texas (DIR), Maryland (COTS master), Pennsylvania (COSTARS).

I found **no sourced evidence** for the common claim that particular states prefer on-premise or in-house systems as a matter of culture. Tyler's 10-K notes that "some of our solutions have not fully achieved nationwide penetration" without naming states [17]. **Do not assert an on-premise-preference geography without primary evidence.**

## 6. Market growth forecasts

### U.S. state/local/education IT — the usable numbers

- **Deltek GovWin:** SLED IT market **$155B in 2025**, growing ~2% that year amid tighter budgets post-ARPA, reaching **$178B by 2028**, averaging ~**4.5% annually** across the 2023–2028 forecast period [45].
- **e.Republic (Beyond the Beltway, presented by Joe Morris and Rachel Eckert):** 2026 state and local IT spending of **$160.2B**, growth of **4–6%** over 2025, broken into verticals: Health and Human Services $44.4B; Education (K-12 + higher ed) $41B; Transportation and Infrastructure $19.2B; Finance and Administration $16.7B; Justice and Public Safety $15B; Utilities $13.5B; Environment and Housing $10.4B [45].
- **TD SYNNEX Public Sector:** corroborates ~4–6% growth to ~$160B in 2026, and adds that **nearly 50% of states are projecting slower revenue growth or general fund declines**, with agencies under pressure to justify new investments [45].

### Regional breakouts — the honest answer

**No public regional forecast for U.S. state/local digital government exists.** Deltek's SLED dataset does segment by all 50 states plus D.C., 8 government types, 12 verticals, 4 major IT segments, and 23 BEA IT categories — but the state-level numbers sit behind a GovWin IQ subscription and are not published [45]. If regional or state-level sizing is needed for the tiering exercise, **buying the Deltek SLED dataset is the direct path**; there is no free substitute.

Global digital-government-services forecasts exist (e.g., ~$55.7B in 2026 to ~$321B by 2035, 18.5% CAGR, North America + Europe ~60% of share) but come from low-transparency market-research publishers with implausible precision [45]. **Do not use these for planning.** They are included only so the team recognizes them when they surface.

## 8. Claims I am least sure about

1. **That Granicus's website/CMS share is really as low as ~1%.** The Replatform Radar `.gov` scan [21] is the best independent measurement available, and its method is unusually transparent, but it covers only `.gov`/`.mil` domains, attributes only 71.2% of reachable sites, and explicitly excludes embedded meeting portals from vendor attribution — which removes Granicus's largest local-government surface. The *relative ordering* (CivicPlus >> Revize >> Granicus in local CMS) I believe. The absolute 1.1% I would not put in a board deck without a second measurement over a non-`.gov` domain sample.

2. **Whether the Washington DES 01313 and Montana state MSA vehicles are actually live.** Both are real, executed, Granicus-named statewide agreements with genuinely valuable terms — Montana's §18 explicitly extends pricing to other states' procurement units, and Washington's cooperative policy does the same [14][31][33]. But the DES page shows a final term end of 11-15-2019 and the Montana MSA shows an initial term through 01-31-2021 [31][14]. Either renewals exist that are not published, or these are historical. **This needs a phone call to WA DES and Montana SITSD before either state is tiered on the strength of them.**

3. **Tyler's active state-portal footprint.** Tyler's 10-K says 30 states with enterprise contracts; Tyler's own brochure says 28; the NIC subsidiary list names ~37 state entities [17]. These cannot all be right simultaneously, and I could not reconcile them to a specific list of currently active statewide portal contracts. The subsidiary list is the least reliable of the three for asserting current competitive presence.

4. **Regional strength attributions for Accela, Revize, and Municode/CivicPlus.** The Accela "strong in California, Florida, and large counties, West Coast metro" characterization comes from a vendor-intelligence blog, not a primary source [37]. The Revize-is-Midwest-concentrated read is my inference from its reference list and Michigan HQ, not from a customer-count-by-state disclosure [19]. The Municode-is-strong-in-the-Southeast read rests on its Tallahassee headquarters and codification franchise [18]. All three are directionally plausible and none is proven.

5. **That Granicus holds no Sourcewell contract and no state municipal league endorsement.** These are negative findings from targeted searching, and negative findings are the weakest kind of evidence. A Sourcewell award could exist under a Granicus subsidiary or legacy brand name I did not search, and a league endorsement could exist without a searchable public page. Both are worth a direct check, because if the Sourcewell gap is real it is the most actionable single finding in this report.
