# State Government Spending Capacity for Digital Transformation — FY2026 and FY2027

Market research input for territory tiering of a Granicus-like govtech SaaS portfolio (digital
communications, website/CMS, meetings and agenda management, public records requests, permitting
and licensing, resident engagement, service request management). Buyers are US state agencies and
large local governments.

**Research date:** September 2026.
**Evidence standard:** Every number below is traceable to a numbered source in Section 7. Where no
source could be found, the cell is left **blank** rather than estimated. Inference is labelled
**[INFERENCE]**.

---

## 1. Big picture

**The 2021–2025 state revenue boom is over, and FY2027 is the first year in which the median state
is budgeting essentially zero real spending growth while simultaneously drawing down the reserves
it built during the boom.** For a govtech vendor, this is not a collapse — it is a shift from
*breadth* to *narrowness*. Money still exists, but it is concentrated in a shrinking number of
states and, within those states, in a shrinking number of named, politically protected programs.

Five things define the period.

**(a) Spending growth has stopped; revenue growth has not quite.** In NASBO's Spring 2026 survey,
governors' recommended FY2027 general fund spending is **down 1.4% in aggregate**, with a **median
of +0.6%** — against a FY2026 aggregate of +7.9% and median of +4.0% (1). General fund *revenue* is
projected up 2.5% in aggregate and 2.5% at the median for FY2027, versus +2.2%/+0.5% for FY2026
(1). Revenue is therefore growing slightly faster than spending, which sounds healthy but actually
reflects deliberate restraint: **23 states are proposing flat or declining general fund spending in
FY2027.** Urban Institute's State Tax and Economic Review put Q1 2026 state tax revenue at **+5.0%
nominal but only +1.7% real** (13) — i.e. most of the apparent growth is inflation.

**(b) Reserves are being spent down on a steep, visible glide path.** Rainy day funds as a share of
general fund expenditures go **14.9% (FY2025 actual) → 13.2% (FY2026 est.) → 11.8% (FY2027 rec.)**
in aggregate; medians are 13.5% → 13.1% → 12.6% (1). Total balances (rainy day funds plus ending
balances) fall harder: from a **$437B peak in FY2023 to $405B in FY2025, $324B in FY2026, and $274B
recommended for FY2027** (1). That is a **37% drawdown from peak in four years.** The practical
consequence for vendors: the one-time money that funded 2022–2025 modernization pilots is largely
committed, and FY2027 is when states must decide whether to move those pilots onto recurring
general fund lines — or kill them.

**(c) A small group of states has genuine structural gaps, and they are large.** NASBO reports
**13 states identified $26.3B in FY2027 budget gaps before balancing actions, and 8 states project
$53.4B in FY2028 gaps** (1). NASBO does not name them, but individually sourced gaps include:
Washington closing a **~$16B** four-year gap partly with ~$4B of new taxes (16); California's LAO
projecting **$20–35B annual** operating deficits through the forecast period (21); Maryland closing
~$1.4B and facing **$2.3B from July 2027 growing toward ~$4B by 2030** (16); Hawai'i a projected
**$3B** shortfall (16); Colorado **>$1.5B** across FY2026–27 (16); Florida **$1.5B+** (16); DC
**$1.1B** plus a **$650M/5yr** federal conformity loss (16); New York **$31.7B** cumulative outyear
gaps (44); Delaware ~**$500M** (16); Oregon a **$755M** emerging shortfall; Louisiana ~**$300M** by
FY2027 (16); Missouri cutting **~$600M** from the general fund. North Carolina faces a potential
**$5B** shortfall if its remaining rate triggers are met (16).

**(d) State IT budgets are flattening faster than state budgets overall.** NASCIO's 2025 State CIO
Survey found **50% of CIO organizations with increased budgets, 32% flat, and 18% decreased** for
FY2026, and while **48% received dedicated modernization funding, 26% of that was one-time money**
(23). The cybersecurity picture deteriorated faster: in the 2026 NASCIO-Deloitte study only **22%
of state CISOs received a 6%+ increase, down from 40% in 2024, while 16% saw cuts, up from 0% in
2024** (25). NASCIO's Top Ten for 2026 put **AI at #1 for the first time**, with budget and cost
control rising (24). Read together: states are reallocating *within* flat IT budgets toward AI and
away from everything else, and the "modernization funding" that does exist is disproportionately
non-recurring.

**(e) Tax cuts have permanently lowered the ceiling in roughly half the country, while a smaller
group is actively raising revenue.** Since 2021, **26 states have cut individual income tax rates,
23 of them top marginal rates, and 13 have cut corporate rates** (26). Rate reductions took effect
**January 1, 2026** in Indiana, Kentucky, Mississippi, Montana, Nebraska, North Carolina, Ohio, and
Oklahoma, with further 2026 enactments in Arkansas, Georgia, South Carolina, Utah, and West
Virginia (26). Several are on **elimination paths** (Mississippi, and active elimination pushes in
Oklahoma and Missouri — though Missouri voters rejected Amendment 5 in August 2026) (16). Georgia's
cuts alone cost **$1.3B** (16). Moving the other way: **Washington's new 9.9% millionaires' tax
(>$3B/yr)** (16, 27), **Hawai'i's new 13% top bracket**, **Maine's 2% surcharge over $1M (~$100M)**,
**Rhode Island's 3% surtax over $1M (~$142M)**, **New York's 7.25% corporate rate extension
($1.3B/yr)**, and **Illinois's $1.8B revenue package** (16).

### What this means for territory tiering

The states worth the most sales capacity are **not** simply the states with the most money. They
are states that combine (i) adequate reserves, (ii) a **named, appropriated, multi-year** technology
program with a funding source that is not one-time, and (iii) a procurement cycle that lets a deal
close inside the fiscal year. On that test:

- **Best combination of capacity and named funding:** New Mexico, Texas, Virginia, Ohio, Georgia,
  Montana. Each has an identifiable multi-year IT funding vehicle *and* the balance-sheet room to
  sustain it.
- **Money exists but the cycle is slow:** Texas, Virginia, Ohio, Montana, North Dakota, Wyoming and
  the other biennial states — a missed session can mean a two-year wait. Montana and Nevada are the
  hardest cases because they hold **no regular legislative session in even-numbered years** (15).
- **High nominal reserves, structurally weak:** Alaska and Wyoming look extraordinary on the rainy
  day metric (51.7% and 70.3% of general fund spending) but are severance-revenue dependent and
  small. Alaska's reserve ratio is recommended to fall to **33.5% in FY2027** (1).
- **Deprioritize on capacity:** Washington (2.8% rainy day, lowest in the nation), New Jersey
  (**0.0%** — the only state with no rainy day balance at all), Illinois (4.5%), Delaware (5.1%),
  Rhode Island (5.2%), Missouri (5.8%), Maryland (7.8%).
- **Structural blockers independent of the cycle:** **Colorado's TABOR** is the only tax and
  expenditure limit in the country that constrains *both* revenue and spending and requires voter
  approval for increases (22, 29); Arizona, California and New Jersey have expenditure limits, and
  14 states require a supermajority for tax increases (29). **Maryland is a special case for
  vendors**: its 3% sales tax on IT and data services is a direct headwind to software purchasing —
  and notably it raised only **$35M against a $500M projection** (16), which makes it both a demand
  drag and a candidate for repeal.

One important caveat that colours the entire table below: **NASBO's FY2027 column is governors'
*recommended* budgets, not enacted ones** (1). Legislatures will move these numbers, and Virginia is
absent from the FY2027 data entirely.

### Total balances (rainy day funds plus general fund ending balance) as % of expenditures, FY2027

The thinnest cushions on a total-balances basis (1): **Washington 3.0, Pennsylvania 6.2, Missouri

### Budget-management strategies invoked for FY2027 (NASBO Table 7)

Across all states, governors' FY2027 recommendations included **3 across-the-board cuts, 22
targeted cuts, 4 layoffs, 1 furlough, 4 benefit cuts, 4 hiring freezes, 14 vacancy eliminations, 8
pension/OPEB changes, 7 rainy day fund withdrawals, 16 fund transfers, 13 uses of prior-year
balances, 14 revenue increases, and 12 Medicaid changes** (1).

**Zero stress levers invoked** (the least-stressed states, and a useful cross-check on the "stable"
classifications): Alabama, Arkansas, Georgia, Iowa, Kentucky, Minnesota, Mississippi, Montana,
North Carolina, North Dakota, Ohio, South Carolina, Texas, Utah, Virginia, Wisconsin, Wyoming.

**Most stress levers invoked:** Missouri (9), Arizona (7), Nebraska (9), Maine (5), Maryland (7),
Colorado (7), New Hampshire (7), Washington (6), West Virginia (6), New Jersey (6), Pennsylvania
(6), Delaware (4).

Note the tension this exposes: **Minnesota and Kentucky pulled no stress levers but are classified
Constrained here**, because their reserve ratios are falling sharply (Kentucky 30.3 → 18.1;
Minnesota 15.9 → 10.9). Conversely **Nebraska pulled many levers despite a 15.2% reserve ratio.**
Table 7 measures political willingness to cut; the reserve ratio measures capacity. They diverge.

## 4. Budget cycle and procurement friction

**Biennial (19):** Connecticut, Hawai'i, Indiana, Kentucky, Maine, Minnesota, Montana, Nebraska,
Nevada, New Hampshire, North Carolina, North Dakota, Ohio, Oregon, Texas, Virginia, Washington,
Wisconsin, Wyoming (15). All other states plus DC are annual.

**No regular legislative session in even-numbered years:** Montana and Nevada (15). These are the
two hardest states to sell a new-money deal into off-cycle; an unbudgeted purchase generally waits
for the next odd-year session or requires a special session.

NASBO characterises the split slightly differently — roughly 30 annual and 20 biennial with one
hybrid — a minor definitional discrepancy (states differ on whether the *legislature* meets
biennially versus whether the *budget* is enacted biennially). Either count supports the same
conclusion.

**Hard spending caps and supermajority requirements.** Between 26 and 31 states have some tax and
expenditure limit depending on the counting methodology, but **Colorado's TABOR is the only one
that limits both revenue and expenditure and requires voter approval to raise taxes** (22, 29).
Arizona, California and New Jersey have expenditure limits. Fourteen states require a
supermajority — commonly three-fifths — to raise taxes (29). Colorado is therefore uniquely
constrained: even when the economy grows, TABOR can force refunds rather than permit discretionary
technology spending, which is consistent with its IT capital request of only $9.8M general fund
plus $17.1M cash funds (30).

**Vendor-specific procurement friction worth flagging:**
- **Maryland** — the 3% sales tax on IT and data services directly raises the cost of software
  purchases. It has badly underperformed, raising **$35M against a $500M projection** (16), which
  makes repeal plausible but the near-term drag real.
- **Florida** — HB 1197 expanded Florida Digital Service oversight of agency technology purchases
  (48). This is a governance change, not a funding increase, and it lengthens approval paths.
- **Virginia** — VITA statewide contracts reduce the utility of national cooperative vehicles,
  since Virginia policy discourages piggybacking where a VITA contract exists.

## 5. Tax policy trajectory

**Cutting (reduces future general fund capacity).** Since 2021: 26 states cut individual income tax
rates, 23 cut top marginal rates, 13 cut corporate rates (26). Reductions effective **January 1,
2026**: Indiana, Kentucky, Mississippi, Montana, Nebraska, North Carolina, Ohio, Oklahoma. Further
2026 enactments: Arkansas, Georgia, South Carolina, Utah, West Virginia (26).

**On elimination paths or with active elimination pushes:** Mississippi (legislated phase-out),
Oklahoma (gubernatorial campaign issue), Missouri (Amendment 5 — **rejected by voters in August
2026**) (16).

**Trigger risk:** North Carolina held its rate at 3.49% for 2027–29, but meeting the remaining
triggers would open a potential **$5B** shortfall (16).

**Raising revenue (expands capacity):** Washington's 9.9% millionaires' tax (**>$3B/yr**) (16, 27);
Hawai'i's new 13% top bracket; Maine's 2% surcharge over $1M (~$100M); Rhode Island's 3% surtax over
$1M (~$142M); New York's 7.25% corporate rate extension (**$1.3B/yr**); Illinois's **$1.8B**
package; New Mexico conformity **+$110M** in FY2028; Delaware **$140M/yr** in business fees (16).

**Federal conformity (OBBBA) is a live, large swing factor.** States that conform to the 2025
federal law lose revenue; those that decouple retain it. DC alone projects a **$650M five-year**
loss from conformity (16). Several states had not resolved conformity at the time of this research,
so FY2027 revenue forecasts in those states carry more error than usual.

## 6. Recommended tiering [INFERENCE]

This section is judgement built on the sourced data above, not itself sourced.

**Tier 1 — invest.** New Mexico, Texas, Virginia, Ohio, Georgia, Montana, Arkansas. Named funding
plus balance-sheet capacity. In the biennial members of this group (Texas, Virginia, Ohio,
Montana), align pipeline to the session calendar rather than to your own fiscal year.

**Tier 2 — maintain.** Alabama, Connecticut, Kansas, Massachusetts, Nevada, North Carolina, North
Dakota, South Dakota, Utah, Wisconsin, Wyoming, Mississippi. Adequate capacity, no identified
program to anchor a large deal. Expect renewals and modest expansion, not transformation.

**Tier 3 — selective.** Minnesota, Pennsylvania, California, New York, Florida, Idaho, Michigan,
Oregon, Oklahoma, South Carolina, Tennessee, West Virginia, Vermont, Kentucky, Louisiana, Iowa,
Indiana, Hawai'i, Maine, New Hampshire, Arizona. Sell against a funded program where one exists
(Minnesota's HSS Modernization Fund, Pennsylvania's cyber line) and against mandate or compliance
drivers elsewhere. Avoid discretionary-spend pitches.

**Tier 4 — minimal.** Washington, New Jersey, Illinois, Delaware, Rhode Island, Missouri, Maryland,
Colorado, DC, Alaska, Nebraska. Either the reserves are not there, a hard cap binds, or a
vendor-specific tax/structural barrier applies. Protect the installed base; do not fund net-new
territory capacity.

**A caution on Tier 4:** distress is not the same as absence of demand. Fiscal pressure frequently
*drives* consolidation, shared services and legacy replacement — but those deals are longer, more
competitive on price, and usually require a documented cost-avoidance case rather than a
capability case.

## 8. Facts I am least sure about

1. **The Pennsylvania CODE PA funding direction is contradictory across sources.** StateScoop
   reported an increase of about **$3.7M**; technical.ly reported a **decrease of roughly $700K**,
   from $11.55M to $10.83M (28, 46). These cannot both be right. They may be measuring different
   things — proposed versus enacted, or the digital services team versus a broader technology line
   — but I could not reconcile them. I have shown the range in the table rather than pick one.

2. **California's IT posture is inferred, not directly sourced for FY2027.** The StateScoop article
   underpinning the "holding the line" characterisation reads as though it describes the **FY2026**
   budget signed in June 2025, not the FY2027 proposal (28). Given California's overall fiscal
   position the direction is very likely right, but treat the specific FY2027 IT claim as inference.

3. **All FY2027 figures in this report are governors' *recommended* budgets, not enacted.** NASBO's
   Spring survey captures proposals (1). Legislatures routinely change them materially. **Virginia
   is missing from the FY2027 data entirely**, and **Georgia, Mississippi and Wisconsin report N/A
   rainy day values**, which is why two cells in the main table fall back to FY2025 actuals.

4. **I could not identify which 13 states make up NASBO's $26.3B of FY2027 gaps.** NASBO publishes
   the aggregate without naming the states (1). Every state-level gap figure in Section 1 therefore
   comes from a *different* source using a *different* definition — some are one-year general fund
   gaps, some are multi-year cumulative, some are pre-balancing and some post. **They are not
   comparable to each other** and should not be summed or ranked against one another.

5. **The rainy day column would look materially different under Pew's methodology.** I cross-checked
   NASBO Table 19 against a reconstruction of Pew's Fiscal 50 reserves data and found significant
   divergence in some states — Idaho is the worst case, at Pew's 148.2 days (≈40.6% of annual
   spending) versus NASBO's 22.2% (1, 9). The two use different fund definitions and different
   vintages. I used NASBO because it publishes exactly the requested metric, percent of general
   fund expenditures, but **any single state's reserve percentage should be verified against that
   state's own documents before it drives a territory decision.**

6. **Annual/biennial counts differ between sources.** NCSL's classification yields 19 biennial
   states (15); NASBO's yields about 20 biennial plus one hybrid. The disagreement is definitional
   rather than factual, but it means the cycle column for a small number of borderline states could
   reasonably be labelled either way.
