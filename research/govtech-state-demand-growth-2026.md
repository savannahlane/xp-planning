# State-by-State Demand Growth for Digital Government Services

**Purpose:** evidence base for tiering US states into higher- vs lower-growth-potential sales territories for a Granicus-like govtech SaaS portfolio (digital communications, web/CMS, agenda and meeting management, records requests, permitting and licensing, resident engagement, short-term rental compliance, service requests).

**Prepared:** September 2026. Primary data are US Census Bureau Vintage 2025 population estimates (released 2026), Census Building Permits Survey through July 2026, 2022 Census of Governments, BLS State and Area Employment through August 2026, and FHFA House Price Index through 2026 Q2.

---

## 1. The big picture

**The single most important change in the demand model this year is that immigration stopped being the growth engine.** National population growth fell from 3,248,745 in the year to July 2024 to 1,781,060 in the year to July 2025 — a 45% drop — and essentially all of the decline is net international migration, which fell from 2,734,468 to 1,262,202. Natural increase was basically unchanged (514,277 → 518,858). All 50 states and DC saw international migration fall year over year; the median decline was 53%.

This matters because, for a large group of states, international migration was the *only* thing keeping population positive. In 2024–25 international migration exceeded total population growth in 19 states and DC; in 13 of those states plus DC the state still grew, meaning without immigration it would have shrunk. New York grew by 1,008 people on the back of 95,634 international migrants offsetting 137,586 domestic out-migrants. Illinois, New Jersey, Massachusetts, Connecticut, Rhode Island, Maryland, Michigan, Oregon, Pennsylvania, Louisiana, Mississippi, Alaska and DC are in the same position. Florida is nearly there: 91% of its 196,680 growth was international migration, and its international inflow fell 37%. **If immigration stays at 2025 levels or falls further, the Northeast, Midwest industrial states, and Florida all move toward flat or negative population.**

**Domestic migration is now the cleaner signal, and it still points South and Mountain West.** South Carolina (+12.0 net domestic migrants per 1,000 residents), Idaho (+9.8), North Carolina (+7.5), Delaware (+6.5), Tennessee (+5.8), Montana (+5.5) and Maine (+5.2) lead. California (-229,077), New York (-137,586), Illinois (-40,017), New Jersey (-37,428) and Massachusetts (-33,340) lose the most. California's total population actually declined in Vintage 2025 (-9,465, -0.02%), the first decline since the post-COVID years and a reversal of the Vintage 2024 vintage's positive reading.

**Housing permitting has rolled over nationally and the decline is concentrated in exactly the Sun Belt states that led growth.** US permitted units fell 3.1% in CY2025 (1,471,367 → 1,426,166) and the trailing 12 months through July 2026 is flat at 1,425,824. Underneath that flat national number is a large regional rotation: the South is down 3.7% year to date in 2026 and the West South Central division down 7.6%, while the Northeast is up 15.6% and the Midwest up 2.3%. Texas (-8.4% YTD), Florida (-13.4%), Arizona (-19.2%) and Nevada (-32.3%) are all contracting hard. New York (+50.5%), Washington (+28.0%), Massachusetts (+27.4%), Montana (+25.8%), Rhode Island (+47.1%) and Kansas (+31.9%) are rebounding off low bases.

**Local government revenue is entering a squeeze, and the squeeze is worst in the fastest-growing states.** NLC's 2025 City Fiscal Conditions report projects general fund revenue down 1.9% in FY2025 with only 45% of finance officers optimistic about FY2026, down from 64%. NASBO's Spring 2026 survey shows proposed FY2027 general fund spending down 1.4% in aggregate, 13 states reporting FY2027 gaps totaling $26.3 billion, and 22 states using targeted spending cuts. Meanwhile the highest-growth territories are precisely where legislatures are capping property tax: Florida's Amendment 3 on the November 2026 ballot would cut local non-school property tax revenue by $4.93 billion in FY2027-28 rising to $11.83 billion by FY2031-32 ($45.84 billion over five years, per the state Revenue Estimating Conference); Texas has committed $51 billion of state budget to property tax relief in 2026-27; North Dakota's HB 1176 added a 3% local levy cap; Nebraska's LB 34 capped property tax growth from FY2026; Colorado's HB24B-1001 set local revenue growth limits; Montana's HB 231/SB 542 created reduced homestead rates; Wyoming voters face a 50% residential exemption initiative in November 2026 estimated to cost local governments at least $188 million by 2030. Kansas is the exception to the pattern in the prompt — its 2026 attempts (HB 2745 local spending cap, SCR 1616 valuation cap) were vetoed or died.

**Property tax base growth has inverted geographically.** FHFA's purchase-only index through 2026 Q2 shows the fastest home-price appreciation in Alaska (+8.3%), Vermont (+7.3%), Hawaii (+5.8%), Illinois (+5.6%) and West Virginia (+5.6%), while California (-0.2%), Colorado (-0.5%), Washington (-0.9%) and New Mexico (-1.3%) are declining and Texas (+0.5%), Florida (+1.0%) and Arizona (+0.6%) are near zero. Assessed values lag market prices by one to three years, so **the Sun Belt property tax base will decelerate into 2027–2028 at the same time as the tax caps bite.**

**Local government headcount is still growing, but barely, and it is not correlated with population growth.** On a smoothed 12-month basis through August 2026, local government employment grew fastest in Hawaii (+3.2%), Michigan (+2.3%), Alabama (+2.2%), Virginia (+2.2%), Maryland (+2.0%) and South Carolina (+1.7%), and shrank in Wisconsin (-1.5%), Rhode Island (-1.4%), Indiana (-0.8%), Tennessee (-0.5%) and Montana (-0.5%). Texas (+0.4%), Florida (-0.1%) and California (0.0%) are flat. This is a notable disconnect: the states adding the most residents are not adding local government staff proportionally, which is a *tailwind* for automation-oriented software (do more with the same headcount) and a *headwind* for seat-based pricing.

### What this means for territory design

1. **The tier-1 growth list is smaller and more Southeastern than the conventional Sun Belt story.** South Carolina, North Carolina, Tennessee, Georgia and Alabama combine strong domestic in-migration, meaningful enterprise account counts, and no large enacted property tax cap. Texas remains tier 1 on absolute scale (87 jurisdictions over 100k, 254 counties, largest permit volume in the country) despite a permit downturn and heavy tax-relief politics.
2. **Florida is the biggest single-state risk in the portfolio.** It is the #2 permit market, has 63 jurisdictions above 100k, and is the natural home for short-term rental compliance revenue — but 91% of its growth is now immigration-dependent, permits are down 13.4%, and Amendment 3 would remove roughly $12 billion of recurring local revenue if approved with 60% of the vote in November 2026. Model Florida with a scenario branch, not a point estimate.
3. **Arizona and Nevada should be downgraded from where they sat two years ago.** Both still show good domestic in-migration, but permits are down 19% and 32% respectively year to date, and both have small enterprise account counts relative to reputation (Nevada has only 7 jurisdictions above 100k; Clark County and Las Vegas are most of the state).
4. **California, New York and Illinois are low *growth* but remain the largest *absolute* markets.** California alone has 112 jurisdictions above 100k — more than Texas and Florida combined have counties above 100k. Tier these as harvest/retention/cross-sell territories, not new-logo growth territories.
5. **Account-count structure matters as much as growth rate.** Idaho grows at 1.44% but has 7 jurisdictions above 100k and 72% of its subcounty governments under 2,500 people. Illinois has 6,930 local governments but 69% of its subcounty units are under 2,500 — a huge list of sub-enterprise accounts. The count of buyable enterprise accounts is concentrated: California (112), Texas (87), Florida (63), North Carolina (39), Georgia (36), New York (34), Pennsylvania (34), Ohio (32).

### 5c. Fiscal condition

## 7. Market maturity: what the commentary actually says

The honest finding is that **there is no widely cited, credible source that describes specific US regions as saturated for local government software.** The dominant framing in vendor and analyst material is that the market is nationally *underpenetrated* and fragmented, with saturation occurring at the *segment* level (largest cities, systems of record) rather than the *geographic* level. The evidence:

**Market size and penetration.** At its June 2026 Investor Day, Tyler Technologies cited a Gartner estimate of a ~$44 billion state, local and K-12 government software market growing at roughly a 10% CAGR since 2023 and expected to continue through the decade. Tyler characterizes its own 50,000+ installations across 16,000+ client locations as approximately 11% share of an estimated 500,000-installation TAM, describing "large underpenetrated whitespace." Tyler serves clients in all 50 states and does not break out geographic saturation.

**Total SLED IT spend.** e.Republic's 2026 forecast puts state and local IT spending at $160.2 billion in 2026, growing 4–6% annually and reaching $202.3 billion by 2030. e.Republic's Joe Morris characterized 2026 as "a market that's much more complex than at any time in recent history," citing fiscal, workforce and modernization pressure simultaneously. Purchasing has shifted toward services (consulting, managed services, systems integration) over pure software and hardware.

**Segment saturation is real at the top of the market.** Granicus reports more than 7,000 public sector customers globally, 30 billion annual digital interactions, and — the most directly relevant saturation datapoint in this report — **48 of the 50 largest US cities**. For a Granicus-like portfolio, the large-city segment is effectively penetrated nationwide; growth has to come from counties, mid-size cities, states, and cross-sell rather than new large-city logos.

**Where saturation language does appear, it is by product category, not geography.** Government Technology's 2026 market outlook distinguishes "highly mature, system-of-record markets" (slower AI adoption, entrenched incumbents) from "constituent-facing or field service markets" used by hundreds or thousands of end users (faster adoption, larger expanding TAM). It also notes that in virtually every govtech segment there is a private-equity-backed or public incumbent holding high share but "viewed as not as innovative as they were 10 years ago."

**Regional vendor strength is the closest thing to a geographic saturation map.** Competitive analysis of mid-size counties (50,000–250,000 population) identifies BS&A Software as strongest in the Midwest and Northeast, and Edmunds GovTech as concentrated in New York, New Jersey, Pennsylvania and Delaware. This implies harder competitive displacement economics in the Midwest and Mid-Atlantic mid-market, not lower demand. *(This source is a vendor-comparison blog and should be treated as directional only.)*

**M&A activity by region.** R.L. Hulett's Q1 2026 GovTech M&A update recorded 110 global deals, 58 in North America, with the Mid-Atlantic most active (16 deals), West Coast 11, Southeast 7, New England 5, all other US regions 9 combined. Deal volume is on pace for 440 deals in 2026 versus 363 in 2025. Mid-Atlantic activity skews federal/defense-adjacent rather than municipal.

**Short-term rental compliance is the clearest *new* demand pocket, and it is bifurcating.** In 2026 two opposite trends are running: cities gaining platform-level enforcement powers (California SB 346 effective Jan 1 2026, compelling Airbnb/Vrbo to hand over per-listing address and parcel data with fines to $10,000/day; Houston Ordinance 2025-322 effective Jan 1 2026 with enforcement from April 1; Austin platform verification from July 1 2026; New Orleans requiring platform permits; New York Local Law 18 plus statewide Article 12-D requiring counties to operate or join STR registries) versus states preempting local authority (Idaho HB 583 and Indiana, both effective July 1 2026). **California, Texas, New York, Louisiana, Nevada and North Carolina are the active STR compliance markets; Idaho and Indiana just got smaller.**

### Practical read

- **Growth markets:** Southeast (SC, NC, GA, TN, AL), Texas, Mountain West (ID, UT, MT), and — for STR/permitting specifically — California and coastal metros regardless of population trend.
- **Mature/harvest markets:** the 50 largest US cities as a segment (Granicus at 48/50); the Northeast and Midwest mid-market where regional incumbents (BS&A, Edmunds) hold entrenched positions; systems-of-record categories generally.
- **The saturation that should worry a Granicus-like vendor is not geographic.** It is that the large-city logo pool is exhausted and the growth has to come from (a) counties, which are more numerous and less penetrated, (b) product attach into existing accounts, and (c) transaction- and usage-funded pricing that bypasses appropriated budgets — the model Tyler explicitly highlighted with its California State Parks contract, which is 100% user-funded rather than state-appropriated.

## 10. The five facts I am least confident about

1. **Florida's demand read.** I have scored Florida "Moderate," but the honest answer is that it is bimodal and the tiering decision should not be made before November 3, 2026. If Amendment 3 passes with 60%, Florida counties and cities lose $4.93B in FY2027-28 growing to $11.83B recurring, with no state backfill, and county commissions absorb 65% of it — while 91% of the state's population growth is already immigration-dependent and permits are down 13.4%. If it fails, Florida is arguably still tier 1 on 63 jurisdictions above 100k and the #2 permit market. I found no polling on the measure. The five-year total is quoted at $45.84B by Florida TaxWatch and the recurring impact at $11.86B by the REC; Ballotpedia cites lower legislative staff estimates ($4.6B FY2027-28, $8.4B FY2028-29), and I did not reconcile the difference.

2. **The permit trend classification for several small states.** DC (+103.9%), Rhode Island (+47.1%), Kansas (+31.9%) and South Dakota (+22.1%) are computed off bases of 1,591, 1,722, 9,995 and 5,371 units respectively. Multifamily permitting is lumpy at these volumes — a single 400-unit project moves DC's number by 25 percentage points. I would not act on the permit column for any state permitting under about 10,000 units a year, which is why those states are marked Low confidence.

3. **Whether the local government employment figures are signal or revision noise.** The August 2026 BLS values are preliminary, and state-level SAE estimates are subject to substantial annual benchmarking revisions. I smoothed to 12-month averages, which helps, but the differences I am reporting between, say, Wisconsin (-1.5%) and Michigan (+2.3%) are within the range that benchmark revisions have moved individual state series in the past. I am confident about the *aggregate* picture (local government headcount roughly flat while population grows) and much less confident about any individual state's rank.

4. **Using FHFA HPI as a property tax base proxy.** I could not obtain current state-by-state assessed-value or local property tax collection data — the Census Annual Survey of State and Local Government Finances lags by roughly two years and the Quarterly Summary does not break local property tax out by state. House price appreciation is a genuine leading indicator, but the translation into assessed value varies enormously by state: California's Proposition 13 caps assessment growth at 2% regardless of market prices; Florida's Save Our Homes caps homestead growth at 3% or CPI; reassessment cycles range from annual to every several years. **The FHFA numbers in §5b should be read as a directional signal about which direction the base is heading, not as a forecast of revenue.**

5. **The market-maturity section rests on thin and partly low-quality sourcing.** Tyler's investor materials and Granicus's own website are solid but self-interested, and Tyler explicitly does not disclose geographic penetration. The claims about regional vendor strength (BS&A in the Midwest/Northeast, Edmunds in the Mid-Atlantic) come from a single vendor-comparison blog. I found no analyst report, market study or investor document that describes any US region as saturated for local government software — which may mean the concept is not how the industry frames it, or may simply mean the relevant research is behind paywalls (Gartner, IDC, e.Republic's full Digital States/Counties datasets) that I could not access. Treat §7 as a hypothesis to validate with a paid market study, not as established fact.

### Two smaller things worth flagging

- **Kansas does not belong on the property-tax-squeeze list** as of September 2026. Both 2026 vehicles failed (HB 2745 vetoed, SCR 1616 died, HCR 5008 amendment rejected 59-62). The premise in the brief appears to have been based on proposed rather than enacted law. Montana, North Dakota, Colorado and Nebraska did enact measures, but I could not find quantified local revenue impact estimates for any of those four.
- **Connecticut, Massachusetts and Rhode Island county counts in the >100k column are misleading.** Connecticut's eight entries are COG planning regions, Massachusetts abolished eight of fourteen county governments, and Rhode Island counties have no government at all. Adjusted for this, Connecticut's real enterprise account pool is closer to 5 (the cities), Massachusetts closer to 9–12, and Rhode Island 1.
