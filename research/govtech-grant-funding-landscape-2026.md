# Grant and External Funding Landscape for State & Local GovTech — 2026

Market research for a Granicus-like govtech SaaS portfolio (digital communications and mass
notification, website/CMS, agenda and meeting management, public records request management,
permitting and licensing, resident engagement, service request management). Buyers are US state
agencies, counties, and large cities.

Research date: September 2026. All figures sourced; see numbered source list at the end.

---

## 1. Big picture

**The direction of federal money into state and local govtech in 2026 is contracting sharply on the
discretionary/flexible side and holding steady-to-growing only on the entitlement/formula side that
funds health and human services case management.** For a vendor selling websites, agendas, records
requests, permitting, and resident engagement, 2026 is the year the money that paid for the
2021–2025 buying wave runs out, and there is no equivalent replacement.

Four things define the year:

**(a) The ARPA cliff is real and dated.** SLFRF obligation deadline was December 31, 2024 (passed).
The expenditure deadline is **December 31, 2026** for nearly all uses, and **September 30, 2026**
for Surface Transportation and Title I projects. Anything not spent by then must be returned to
Treasury. Only administrative closeout costs may be paid after that, through the final Project &
Expenditure report due April 30, 2027 (1, 2, 3). As of March 31, 2025 — the most recent complete
data in GAO's February 2026 review — states had spent $156.3B of $195.8B (80%) and localities
$107.2B of $127.8B (84%), leaving roughly **$60B nominally unspent but essentially all obligated**
(4). Unobligated money was trivial: $10.4M across 11 states and $101M across 2,397 localities;
Treasury had recovered $13.7M as of November 2025 (4).

The mechanism that matters for govtech is **revenue replacement**. 53% of state spending and 67% of
local spending went through that category, and 78% of localities put their *entire* award through
it; NEUs (small towns under 50,000) put 88% of their money there (4). Revenue replacement was
effectively a general-fund top-up, and it is where websites, permitting portals, agenda systems,
records digitization, and mass-notification subscriptions were bought without ever being labeled as
"technology" in Treasury reporting. **That is why no clean state-by-state "ARPA technology spend"
number exists — and why the cliff is broader than any technology line item suggests.** What breaks
in 2027 is not new purchases; it is *renewals*, because a multi-year SaaS subscription bought with
one-time federal money has no recurring funding source behind it.

**(b) The IIJA digital programs have been restructured or killed.** BEAD survived but was rewritten:
NTIA's June 2025 Restructuring Policy Notice forced technology-neutral, lowest-cost "Benefit of the
Bargain" bidding, all 56 Final Proposals were approved by early-to-mid 2026, and the reforms
generated **$21B in "savings" out of the $42.45B program** — roughly half (5, 6, 7). In September
2026 NTIA issued a Supplemental Deployment Policy Notice directing part of those savings to a
*second* bidding round for newly identified unserved locations, and explicitly left non-deployment
uses unresolved (5, 8). The practical read for govtech: BEAD money goes to ISPs, not to government
software, and the non-deployment bucket that digital-inclusion and state-capacity advocates hoped
for is still not authorized.

The **Digital Equity Act is mostly dead**. Trump announced cancellation on May 8, 2025; Commerce
terminated the Competitive Grant Program on May 9 and terminated the State Digital Equity Planning
and Capacity Grants by letter (9, 10). On July 15, 2026, Judge Bates (D.D.C., *NDIA v. Trump*) held
that including racial/ethnic minority status as a "covered population" is an unconstitutional racial
classification **but severable**, and the government committed to reinstating the Competitive
Program; Commerce told the court on August 3, 2026 that it "presently targets a December 2026
opening date," and opened a comment period August 25 (comments closed October 26, 2026) (9, 10, 11,
12). **The $1.25B Competitive Program may come back; the State Capacity Grants — the money that went
to state broadband offices — remain canceled with no ruling and no timeline** (9, 11). Concrete
state losses: California $70M, New York $36M, Michigan $20.5M (13, 14).

**(c) The State and Local Cybersecurity Grant Program has functionally lapsed.** Authority expired
September 30, 2025. A CR revived it, and the Consolidated Appropriations Act, 2026 (P.L. 119-75,
enacted February 3, 2026) extended authorization through September 30, 2026 — **with no new
appropriation attached** (15, 16). FY2025 was the last funded cycle at **$91.75M nationwide, down
from $400M+ in cycle one, with the local match at 40%** (16). The House passed the PILLAR Act
(H.R. 5078) by unanimous consent on November 18, 2025 to reauthorize through FY2033; the Senate
companion (S.3251, Hassan/Cornyn, $300M for FY2026) has not moved out of committee (16, 17, 18).
NASCIO is now asking appropriators for $300M in **FY2027** (19). CISA has told states to plan for a
future without the grant (16). Practical effect: shrinking dollars, a match most small jurisdictions
cannot meet, and a scramble by states to backfill.

**(d) The countervailing force is compliance and entitlement-funded modernization, not grants.**
Three things are genuinely expanding:

- **ADA Title II web/mobile accessibility.** DOJ's April 2024 rule requires WCAG 2.1 Level AA. An
  Interim Final Rule published April 20, 2026 **extended** the deadlines by one year: **April 26,
  2027** for entities with population 50,000+, **April 26, 2028** for under-50,000 and all special
  district governments (20, 21). The rule explicitly covers content provided "through contractual,
  licensing, or other arrangements" — i.e., vendor-hosted CMS. This is the single strongest
  non-grant demand driver for a website/CMS vendor in 2026–2028, and Massachusetts has already made
  it the top scoring priority in its municipal IT grant program (22, 23).
- **Medicaid eligibility and enrollment.** OBBBA (P.L. 119-21, July 4, 2025) §71119 requires
  community-engagement (work) requirements by January 1, 2027 plus 6-month redeterminations. CMS
  guidance (December 8, 2025; further guidance June 1, 2026) confirms **$200M in FY2026 "Government
  Efficiency Grants"** — $100M split equally across 50 states + DC, $100M by share of affected
  population — **on top of the standing 90% federal match for design/development/installation and
  75% for maintenance and operations** of Medicaid E&E systems, which remain fully intact (24, 25,
  26, 27, 28). Nebraska implemented first on May 1, 2026. This is the largest active federally
  funded state-systems build in the country, and it drags notices, correspondence, document upload,
  and case-management work with it.
- **FEMA preparedness grants are up, not down.** FY2026 HSGP totals **$1.064B** (up from $1.008B in
  FY2025): SHSP $394.25M, UASI $584.25M, OPSG $85.5M. At least 30% must go to five National Priority
  Areas, which include **cybersecurity** and **election security** (3% minimum) (29, 30). FY2026 EMPG
  is **$337.25M**, up from $319.5M (31, 32). **BRIC was unlawfully terminated and then restored by
  court order**: permanent injunction December 11, 2025; enforcement order March 6, 2026; FEMA issued
  a combined FY2024–FY2025 NOFO totaling **$1B** (33, 34). HMGP has no fixed appropriation — it is
  disaster-triggered as a percentage of assistance — and is therefore a pipeline you cannot forecast.

**Streams that were cut or paused.** DOL terminated **all** state ARPA-funded UI grants on May 22,
2025, including IT modernization awards; no replacement has been announced, and DOL's OIG opened an
audit in April 2026 of how states spent the $204M in UI IT modernization funds, with a report due
Q3 FY2027 (35, 36, 37). Wisconsin's $29M termination is the best-documented case and has not been
reversed despite repeated requests from the governor (38, 39). **SNAP is moving the wrong way for
state HHS tech budgets**: OBBBA §10106 cuts the federal share of SNAP administrative costs from 50%
to 25% beginning **FY2027 (October 1, 2026)** — a proposed rule to codify it published June 24,
2026 — and from FY2028 states with payment error rates at or above 6% must pay 5–15% of *benefit*
costs (40, 41, 42). That is a direct raid on the same state agency budgets that buy case management.
**Child support enforcement is stable but unglamorous**: 66% federal financial participation on
allowable Title IV-D costs including APD-approved automated systems, open-ended with no ceiling —
but the 80%/90% enhanced rates are long-expired PRWORA-era provisions, not current (43, 44, 45).
**USDA Rural Development still works for small rural jurisdictions**: Community Facilities Direct
Loans & Grants accept applications year-round and can fund equipment including public-safety and
telecommunications end-user equipment, at 15–75% grant share depending on population and income
(46, 47). Community Connect had $17M in FY2026 (applications closed June 29, 2026) (48). **There is
no funded federal NG911 grant program** — H.R. 6505 is pending in House Energy & Commerce with no
roll-call votes and authorizes only "such sums as necessary" (49, 50).

**State fiscal backdrop.** NASBO's Spring 2026 Fiscal Survey shows governors' FY2027 budgets with
**median general fund spending growth of 0.6%** and revenue growth of 2.5%; 22 states proposed
targeted cuts, 14 planned to eliminate vacant positions; total balances are declining as one-time
surpluses are spent down (51, 52). States are not in crisis, but they have no appetite for new
recurring commitments — which is exactly the wrong posture for absorbing expiring ARPA-funded SaaS
subscriptions.

### Where the ARPA technology cliff bites hardest

Three groups, in order of severity:

1. **States GAO flagged as slow spenders.** Six states had spent **less than half** their SLFRF
   awards as of March 31, 2025 (27%–48%): **Mississippi, New Jersey, Oklahoma, South Carolina,
   Tennessee, West Virginia** (4). They are still spending federal money through calendar 2026 and
   fall off the hardest on January 1, 2027.
2. **Small-population states**, because ARPA's formula gave every state a $500M floor regardless of
   size. Alaska ($1.01B), North Dakota ($1.01B), Montana ($906M), Delaware ($925M), Maine ($997M),
   New Hampshire ($995M), Vermont, Wyoming, South Dakota, Rhode Island all received extraordinary
   per-capita amounts, and their locals funded disproportionately large tech programs from it (53).
   North Dakota is the extreme case: GAO reports **100% of its reported spending went to revenue
   replacement** (4).
3. **Non-entitlement units of local government everywhere** — the ~26,352 small cities and towns
   that received $20.2B and put 88% of it through revenue replacement (4). This is the base of the
   website/CMS/agenda market. It is a nationwide cliff, not a regional one, and it differentiates
   states only by how much own-source revenue their small jurisdictions have to absorb renewals.

Conversely, the states that already ran their money out — **California, District of Columbia,
Massachusetts, and New York reported spending their entire SLFRF awards** as of March 31, 2025 (4) —
have largely absorbed the transition already and are less exposed to a 2027 shock.

### What this means for territory tiering

- **Buy signal moving forward is compliance, not grants.** ADA Title II (April 2027 / April 2028) and
  Medicaid work-requirement implementation (January 2027) are mandates with deadlines, and mandates
  survive budget cuts better than discretionary modernization.
- **The highest-value state programs for this portfolio are small and specific**, not large and
  broad: Massachusetts Community Compact IT, New York LGRMIF, Colorado SIPA GovGrants/Micro-Grants,
  Florida's Local Government Cybersecurity Grant Program, Maryland's Local Cybersecurity Support
  Fund, New Jersey LEAP. These fund exactly the SKUs in this portfolio.
- **Purchasing vehicles are worth as much as grants in low-aid states.** Texas has the lowest-but-4
  state aid to locals in the country ($170/capita) and simultaneously the best cooperative
  purchasing infrastructure (DIR contracts + Texas SmartBuy). Contract access reduces sales cycle
  length even where there is no grant money (54, 55, 56, 57).
- **Watch for state backfill of SLCGP.** Virginia, Washington, Minnesota, Maryland, New York,
  Massachusetts, and Utah are the clearest examples of states putting state money or state shared
  services behind local cyber as the federal grant winds down (58, 59, 60, 61, 62).

## 2. State-by-state table

Ratings are analyst judgments, not published rankings.

- **Funding environment** — "rich" means at least one named, recurring, state-funded program or
  authority that directly buys local-government digital services, *or* an exceptional statewide
  purchasing vehicle plus high state aid. "Moderate" means federal pass-throughs plus usable
  statewide contracts, but no dedicated state local-tech grant. "Thin" means no dedicated program,
  low state aid to locals, and locals funding technology from own-source revenue.
- **ARPA tech cliff exposure** is inferred from two observable proxies — GAO's March 2025 spend-pace
  data (4) and per-capita SLFRF allocation (53) — because no state-level "ARPA technology spend"
  dataset exists. Confidence is stated accordingly.
- "State aid/cap" figures are FY2024 Census state intergovernmental payments to local governments
  per capita (63); they are dominated by school funding formulas and by whether a state centralizes
  services, so they are a weak signal used only as one input.

## 4. The facts I am least sure about

1. **Every "ARPA technology cliff exposure" rating in the table.** There is no published dataset of
   SLFRF spending on technology by state. Treasury's project-level data exists, but because 53% of
   state and 67% of local spending ran through revenue replacement — where projects are reported as
   generic "government services" — technology purchases are largely invisible in it. My ratings are
   inferred from two proxies (GAO spend-pace and per-capita allocation) and should be treated as
   hypotheses to validate against your own renewal book, not as findings. The six GAO-identified
   slow-spender states (MS, NJ, OK, SC, TN, WV) are the one part of this column I would defend.

2. **The "rich / moderate / thin" classification for roughly 20 middle states.** For states where I
   found no dedicated local-government technology grant program, I cannot distinguish "no program
   exists" from "no program surfaced in search." Absence of evidence here is weak evidence of
   absence — particularly for Arizona, Indiana, Iowa, Kentucky, Nebraska, Oregon, and the Dakotas,
   where state IT agencies may run local shared-services or grant programs that are poorly indexed.
   The states I am confident about are the ones with named programs and dollar figures.

3. **Whether the Digital Equity Competitive Grant Program actually reopens in December 2026, and
   whether the State Capacity Grants ever come back.** Commerce's December target is a litigation
   filing, not a commitment, and the court's severability reasoning was never applied to the Capacity
   Program. The Solicitor General's March 2026 letter asserts both programs are constitutionally
   defective. I would not plan revenue against either.

4. **SLCGP's FY2027 outcome.** The authorization runs through September 30, 2026, the House passed
   PILLAR, the Senate has not moved, and NASCIO is asking for $300M in FY2027. Any of "fully
   reauthorized and funded," "extended again with no money," or "lapsed entirely" is plausible. The
   $91.75M FY2025 figure and the 40% match are solid; the forward path is not.

5. **The per-capita state aid figures (column note in the table).** These come from a secondary
   aggregator of Census Annual Survey of State Government Finances FY2024 data rather than from
   Census directly, and the metric itself is badly confounded — Utah at $61 and Hawaii at $84 reflect
   centralized service delivery, not stingy states. I used it only as a tiebreaker and would verify
   against Census source tables before putting it in front of a customer.

**One additional caveat worth flagging:** the New York LGRMIF total is reported inconsistently at the
source. The State Archives program page says "65 grants totaling $3.8 million"; the same agency's
press release and press coverage say $4.1 million to 65 institutions. I used $4.1M in the table and
noted the discrepancy here.
